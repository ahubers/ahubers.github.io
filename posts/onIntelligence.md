# A summary of my concerns including but not limited to large language models, the future of humanity and the planet as a whole

This blog post details a few opinions I would like jotted down and propulsed from my head. I have never written a blog post before---nor do I, in particular, even read them. So take these thoughts for as much value as they have (that is: very little). 

My thesis can be stated concisely as follows:

> Proponents of large language models misunderstand large language models in a way wholly analogous to how large language models misunderstand. 

In particular, proponents of large language models have an *extensional* understanding of what a large language model is: a black box that accepts inputs and ejects (often extraordinary) outputs. Likewise, a large language model itself has an *extensional* "understanding" of what it describes: a mapping of outputs most closely (probabilistically) corresponding to their inputs.

I would like to begin to make my case by recalling, as an analogy, the `n-1'th` revolution to the technology industry:

## What even is a block chain?

I have a shameful admission: I do not understand what a block chain or a bitcoin is. More accurately: I cannot describe block chains with the same depth with which I could describe other rudimentary topics in Computer Science---say, algorithmic complexity, linked lists, recursion, binary trees, and so on. My comprehension is more composed of a simplified analogy, which goes as follows:


> Imagine you and 99 friends strip yourselves of the US dollar and move to a remote village. Finding a need for currency still, you (as a group) decide to instead each keep track of how much "money" each other has by individually recording each villager's wealth on private ledgers. Each villager is the sole maintainer of their ledger. This system seems to work---the village is even able to catch "cheaters" who misreport their wealth on their own ledgers, as the village requires all the ledgers to agree. A large pain point, however, does arrive: whenever any two villagers engage in a transaction, all 100 villagers must update their ledgers. This is a chore! The village concocts the following plan to mitigate this chore. First, transactions will be batched together into "blocks" and buffered until a village-wide update next occurs. In order to initiate a village-wide update to ledgers, a big hole must be dug. Requiring some form of "Proof of Work" secures the system because a cheater would have to dig faster than every other villager combined to force a fraudulent entry. The first person to finish digging a big hole earns the right to update the village's ledgers, and is rewarded with new (concocted from thin air) "money" and also small, taxed percentages of the recorded transactions.

To the best of my (as already admitted, poor) understanding: the analogy holds by substituting "private ledgers" for "block chain", buffers of pending transactions for "blocks", and "digging holes in the ground" for "bitcoin mining."

Here are some natural follow-up questions for which I do not have an answer:
- The ledger is a "block chain"---how is it stored on a computer? In what format? 
- Is a block chain a data structure that can be specified theoretically (e.g., like a stack or queue), or is it coupled in some way to its implementation (e.g., like a linked list or an ArrayList)?
- What are the computational complexities of common operations on a block chain (e.g., updating a ledger or appending a new transaction)?
- Where is a block chain stored? By whom?
- What distributed algorithms are used to ensure a consensus of ledgers? On what network would such an algorithm run?

Admittedly, I did not begin to understand block chain at all until the analogy above was relayed to me by a colleague. On spare, brief occasions, I would try to find primary sources to answer the above questions. I would usually come up short, or feel out of my depth.

This brings us to, I suppose, the rhetorical backbone of this post: if I don't get this stuff, how is it everyone else seems to?
